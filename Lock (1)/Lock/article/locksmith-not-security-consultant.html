Your Locksmith Is Not a Physical Security Consultant

Here is a piece of advice I like to share whenever I have the opportunity: your locksmith is not a physical security consultant.

If you look at the courses required for a vocational diploma in locksmithing, none really focuses on security itself. [1] Instead, they cover the installation of different types of locks and security systems, customer relations, and health and safety. Locksmiths are not expected to know every vulnerability in the systems they install, and that is understandable. Their job is to install those systems and prepare the charts needed to design coherent master key systems. The workers building a bridge do not need to understand all the structural calculations performed by the civil engineer in order to build it.

Assessing those vulnerabilities is the role of a physical security consultant. Yet there is no clearly defined educational path into that profession. The field is something of a jungle: it is unregulated, and anyone can call themselves a “security expert” without having to demonstrate the expertise that title implies.

Becoming a physical security consultant means proving your abilities by uncovering vulnerabilities, gaining hands-on experience, continually researching new ways to bypass security systems, and developing an in-depth understanding of how they work.

In my experience, this requires dedication and passion. No security company—whether Ilco, BEST, Corbin, or another manufacturer—is going to pay you to investigate its products' vulnerabilities and expose them publicly. They will, however, be quite happy to pay for your locksmith training so that you can sell their products.

ALOA Security Professionals Association, Inc., historically known as the Associated Locksmiths of America, offers professional locksmith certifications, including Certified Registered Locksmith (CRL), Certified Professional Locksmith (CPL), and Certified Master Locksmith (CML).[2]

However, ALOA receives support from companies in the lock and security industry. The partners shown include McGrath Smart Locks, TURN 10 Wholesale Service, SECLOCK, SDC, Medeco (ASSA ABLOY), Schlage, and DESLOC.[3]

In my view, it is difficult to imagine that certification programs supported by these companies would be intended to encourage the discovery of new ways to bypass their own security systems. My point is that it is not necessarily in those companies' commercial interests for locksmiths to become aware of their products' vulnerabilities, especially when those vulnerabilities cannot be remedied.

I also want to point out that, if we recognize the need for cybersecurity professionals, why not for physical security professionals as well?
