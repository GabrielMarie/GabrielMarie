Trust

Giving an employee a master key is a significant expression of trust. But that trust should work both ways: the employer must trust the employee to handle the key responsibly, and the employee must trust that the employer's security requirements are reasonable and worth following. Handing master keys to custodians employed by a third-party company, without ensuring that the company understands the building's security arrangements and has trained its staff accordingly, is not informed trust. It is blind trust.

Trust is not necessarily the best basis for making decisions, especially when there is a substantial amount of money on the other side of a vault door. Yet, faced with the threat of malicious uses of AI, we must also question the assurances offered by companies worth billions of dollars. Some market their systems as ``ultra-secure,'' while documents very similar to this one, sold on the black market or simply available online, explain in detail how to bypass them.

At a time when a student living on just CAD~15,000 per year can defeat systems designed by such companies, trust between people seems to me to remain one of the last lines of defence. I do not think we can trust the security industry when so many of its products rely on people being unaware of their vulnerabilities.

This is where mutual trust in the workplace matters. Employees need more than instructions: they need to understand why those instructions matter, and they need to trust the people asking them to follow them. Protecting a master key should matter just as much to a student working as a custodian, with many other things on their mind, as it does to the person responsible for managing the building's keys.

For example, a custodian asked to keep their keys secured to their person should understand that this is a security measure, not an arbitrary rule. They may not know that some keys can be reproduced from a photograph, but they should be given a clear explanation of the precautions expected of them. Trust in the person setting those requirements can support compliance; it should not replace training. A single lapse in attention may compromise an entire system, so the organization must make safe practices understandable and practical.

I also want to be clear that I have a great deal of respect for custodians. My concern is not that they are inherently less trustworthy than other employees. Rather, my experience has led me to question the level of responsibility placed on them and the conditions under which they are expected to carry it. Their work can be demanding, repetitive, and complex, leaving their attention divided among many tasks.

Possessing a master key is a substantial responsibility, and compensation, training, and working conditions should reflect that responsibility. Yet, in my experience, custodians are not always given that recognition or support. It is difficult to justify making the security of facilities worth millions of dollars depend on someone earning barely more than minimum wage, working nights, and managing an already demanding workload. Night work may also offer fewer opportunities to build relationships with the people who use the building, making it harder to develop the sense of belonging that employers may nevertheless expect.

The central issue, then, is not the custodian who makes a mistake. It is the organizational decision to grant broad access without providing support proportionate to the responsibility. If an employer chooses to entrust someone with a master key, it must also accept responsibility for the training, resources, compensation, and working conditions that make that trust reasonable.
